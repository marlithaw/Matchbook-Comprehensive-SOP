Campus Culture Operations Manual

Safe · Respectful · Responsible

Adult procedures for teaching, reinforcing, repairing, protecting, and improving Safe · Respectful · Responsible culture at Matchbook Learning. This is the operating manual for the daily adult moves that make culture visible and reliable — not a philosophy statement, not a file inventory.

Core operating question: How does Matchbook teach, reinforce, repair, protect, and improve culture every day?

Culture commitment: Students do not experience adult intentions. They experience adult routines, language, recognition, correction, repair, documentation, and follow-through.
Campus Operating Map

How Culture Actually Runs

Matchbook culture is taught through daily practice, reinforced through belonging and recognition, repaired through restorative response, protected through student support and required procedures, and improved through adult coaching and data review.

TeachModel, practice, rehearse, and reteach expectations in the setting where students must use them.
ReinforceRecognize behavior tied to Safe, Respectful, Responsible and belonging.
RepairRespond to harm or disruption with dignity, regulation, accountability, and return-to-learning.
ProtectEscalate supports when students need more; preserve rights, safety, and access.
ImproveReview adult practice and student experience data to strengthen implementation.
Restorative anchorRemoval is not the intervention. The intervention is the sequence: notice, adult regulation, least-invasive prompt, reteach or reset, supported reset if needed, repair after regulation, re-entry, documentation, and pattern review.
Manual standardUse the least intensive effective support first. Escalate only when student need, safety, or instructional access requires a stronger response. Escalation is never a shortcut around teaching, relationship, regulation, family partnership, documentation, or repair.
Part 1

The Culture Promise

Define the adult standard that makes Safe, Respectful, and Responsible culture visible in every classroom, hallway, common space, family communication, and support decision. Culture at Matchbook is the shared adult operating system that helps students belong, learn, repair harm, lead, and remain connected to instruction.

Operating standardAdults are responsible for the conditions students experience. When a pattern emerges, adults review the routine, adult response, setting, access, and relationship conditions before concluding the student is unwilling to meet expectations.
1.1

Apply the Culture Promise to Adult Decisions

Owner: Every campus adult; leaders coach and monitor consistency.

  • Name the expectation using Safe, Respectful, or Responsible language.
  • Teach or reteach the replacement behavior before relying on consequence.
  • Use the least intensive effective response first.
  • Protect the student from public shame or power struggles.
  • Document significant patterns; review for adult practice, not just student behavior.
Equity checkAdults do not treat dialect, processing time, disability-related behavior, trauma response, cultural communication style, or language acquisition as automatic disrespect.
1.2

Protect Dignity During Correction

Owner: The adult closest to the moment.

  • Regulate face, posture, voice, and pace before speaking.
  • Use proximity or a private cue before public correction.
  • Name the routine, not the student.
  • Give one clear next step; walk away when the student has a path back.
Equity checkMonitor whether certain students receive public correction more often than peers, and adjust adult practice accordingly.
1.3

Use Equity as an Operating Practice

Owner: Every adult owns daily equity; leaders own monitoring of patterns.

  • Teach expectations with visuals, examples, and grade-band language.
  • Check understanding before assigning intent.
  • Separate behavior impact from assumptions about motivation.
  • Review recognition, correction, referral, and exclusion patterns by group.
Equity checkRecognition cannot privilege only loud, compliant, already-visible students — it must notice growth, repair, self-regulation, and help-seeking.
1.4

Use the Shared Adult Language

Owner: All adults; leaders calibrate during coaching.

  • Use Safe, Respectful, Responsible as the main vocabulary.
  • Frame expectations as observable actions.
  • Avoid coded or vague phrases (attitude, disrespectful, defiant, lazy) unless paired with observable facts.
  • Use the same language in family communication.
Equity checkCheck whether language patterns differ across race, gender, disability, language, or grade band.
Part 2

Teach the Culture

Students should not have to guess what success looks like. Adults teach culture the same way they teach academics — models, practice, feedback, reteaching, and repeated opportunities to succeed in the real setting.

Operating standardCorrection should not be the first time a student learns the expectation. When a routine is not visible, adults reteach the routine before treating the behavior as refusal.
2.1

Launch Expectations Through Culture Camp

When: Start of year, new cohort entry, after breaks, or a campus reset.

  • Teach Safe, Respectful, Responsible as observable behaviors in each setting.
  • Model each routine before asking students to perform it independently.
  • Practice routines in the actual spaces where they occur.
  • Introduce LiveSchool, Houses, GiveThx, reset tools, and repair language.
2.2

Run the First Five

Owner: Classroom teacher.

  • Greet students at the threshold or meeting area.
  • Direct students to the first task or readiness routine.
  • Scan for access, regulation, and belonging needs before instruction begins.
  • Begin instruction on time once the room is ready.
2.3

Use Morning Meeting Culture Lab

Owner: Classroom teacher; grade leads calibrate.

  • Open with a brief regulation or grounding practice.
  • Preview the value, routine, or relationship skill for the day.
  • Use a role-play, gratitude move, or repair phrase.
  • Close with a clear transition into academics.
2.4

Teach and Reteach Schoolwide Routines

When: Launch, after breaks, or when data shows slippage.

  • Name the setting and expectation.
  • Show a model in the actual space; name a non-example without using a student.
  • Let students practice immediately; give brief feedback.
  • Reteach when the routine is not yet secure.
2.5

Use the Reteach as the Immediate Consequence

Owner: The adult responsible for the setting.

  • Pause the routine without sarcasm or public blame.
  • Reset the group; model or narrate the expected action.
  • Practice again immediately; recognize evidence once it improves.
2.6

Calibrate Grade-Band Expectations

Owner: Grade teams with culture leadership.

  • Translate Safe, Respectful, Responsible into grade-appropriate language.
  • Use visuals for younger students, explicit rationale for older students.
  • Agree on shared replacement-behavior language across adults.
2.7

Teach Common-Space Culture

When: Arrival, hallway, restroom, cafeteria, recess, assembly, dismissal.

  • Teach the routine in the actual space; post visible cues.
  • Position adults where behavior is most likely to drift.
  • Recognize evidence before correcting missteps.
  • Report repeated patterns to the culture team.
Part 3

Reinforce Belonging & Identity

Recognition should not reward only quiet compliance. It should make visible belonging, effort, repair, leadership, help-seeking, self-regulation, gratitude, growth, and contribution.

Operating standardRecognition is evidence, not decoration. Adults should be able to tell students what behavior was recognized and why it matters to the community.
3.1

Use LiveSchool for the Behavior Economy

Owner: All adults award points; leadership calibrates categories.

  • Award points for observed evidence tied to a taught expectation.
  • Use House points to strengthen collective identity.
  • Enter behavior evidence after stabilization, not during escalation.
  • Review data for disproportionality and invisibility.
3.2

Use GiveThx for Gratitude & Belonging

Owner: Teachers facilitate; staff model adult-to-adult gratitude.

  • Teach what meaningful appreciation sounds like, with sentence stems.
  • Review who is giving and receiving appreciation.
  • Use data to identify students who need intentional connection.
3.3

Operate Houses & Cardinal Cash

Owner: Culture leadership owns design; all staff reinforce.

  • Connect House identity to values, not competition alone.
  • Award points for values-based evidence.
  • Use rewards to amplify intrinsic identity, not replace it.
3.4

Prepare & Run Friday Cypher

Owner: Culture leadership plans; teachers nominate.

  • Identify the week's culture focus and evidence to notice.
  • Include evidence of growth, repair, and leadership — not only frequent standouts.
  • Keep the celebration tight, joyful, and values-aligned.
3.5

Select VIP & Student of the Week

Owner: Teachers nominate; culture leadership calibrates.

  • Use value-based evidence, not adult preference or popularity.
  • Consider growth, repair, leadership, effort, and community support.
  • Review whether nominations reflect the full student body.
3.6

Use Recognition Data to Improve Adult Practice

Owner: Culture leadership, grade leads, administrators.

  • Review who receives positive recognition and who does not.
  • Compare recognition with correction and referral patterns.
  • Set one adult practice adjustment for the next cycle.
Part 4

Respond & Repair

When behavior interrupts learning or harms the community, adults protect instruction without severing belonging. The response is not a place — it is a sequence of adult moves that begins with regulation and ends with return-to-learning, documentation, and pattern review.

Operating standardA student may leave the classroom temporarily only when the student or learning environment needs support that cannot safely or effectively happen in the room. The purpose is regulation, repair, return-to-learning, and pattern review — never exclusion.
4.1

Use the Least-Invasive Classroom Response

  • Regulate yourself before correcting the student.
  • Nonverbal cue, proximity, or quiet prompt first.
  • Offer a choice between two safe next steps.
  • Return attention to instruction quickly.
4.2

Run the Live Intervention Diagnostic

Check rigor (under-challenged?), access (missing materials/scaffolds?), regulation (outside window of tolerance?), safety (immediate risk?) — then match the response to the need instead of defaulting to removal.

4.3

Use In-Class Reset

  • Teach reset options before students need them.
  • Offer quietly and neutrally with a short time limit.
  • Provide simple tools: breathing card, sensory object, quiet seat.
  • Invite the student back with a clear next action.
4.4

Request Restorative Team Push-In

  • Teacher gives brief, factual information only.
  • Support adult diagnoses access, rigor, regulation, or safety need.
  • If the student can remain in class, support in place.
  • Return the student with a clear re-entry step.
4.5

Use Supported Reset Outside the Classroom

  • Calm, low-word transition language — do not process in the hallway.
  • Begin with regulation, not interrogation.
  • Return the student with a clear entry sentence or teacher handoff.
4.6

Operate the Regulation Station & Restorative Room

  • Receive the student with calm, neutral language.
  • Prioritize regulation before reasoning or reflection.
  • Guide reflection: what happened, who was affected, what repair is needed.
  • Coordinate with the teacher for re-entry.
4.7

Conduct Teacher-Led Repair

  • Wait until both student and adult are regulated.
  • Ask what happened, who was affected, what next step repairs the harm.
  • Agree on the re-entry sentence or plan.
4.8

Create the Re-Entry Plan

  • Confirm the student is regulated enough to return.
  • Name the next expected behavior; plan the teacher greeting.
  • Identify the check-in adult and time.
4.9

Use Restorative Re-Entry Center

For extended support: ISS-coded response or when the team determines supervised support is needed to protect safety and learning.

  • Begin with regulation; ensure academic access.
  • Provide restorative reflection only after regulation.
  • Complete re-entry planning before the student returns.
4.10

Move From Live Card to LiveSchool & Official Documentation

  • Manage the moment first — do not stop dysregulation to write a long record.
  • After stabilization, enter evidence in LiveSchool or official systems as required.
  • Write observable facts; avoid labels, motives, and diagnoses.
4.11

Review Patterns After Repeated Response

  • Review setting, time, task, adult, peer dynamics, and prior support.
  • Determine whether the pattern needs Tier 1 reteach, Tier 2/3 support, or safety procedure.
  • Assign an owner and review date.
Part 5

Intensify Support & Protect Rights

Some students need more than universal culture supports. Intensifying support should make the system more precise, not more punitive — understand need, teach replacement skills, coordinate adult response, involve families, document accurately, protect rights, and return students to the least restrictive successful support as soon as appropriate.

Operating standardA higher tier is not a label. It is a coordinated set of adult actions, progress measures, family communication, documentation, and review dates.
Behavior MTSS at a Glance
TierFocusMovement Rule
Tier 1Universal teaching, reteach, gatekeeper checklist before any Tier 2 consideration.Complete Tier 1 fidelity before escalation — safety/special-entry criteria may fast-track review.
Tier 2CICO / DBRC, mentor check-ins, weekly progress review.Team decision — continue, revise, fade, exit, or intensify — never one adult's frustration.
Tier 3Behavior contracts, BSP, FBA/BIP, safety plans, Aspire referral.Least intensive effective tool first; reviewed at least weekly for active high-risk students.
5.1–5.2

MTSS Decision Rules & Tier 1 Gatekeeping

  • Define the concern in observable terms; review multiple data sources.
  • Confirm Tier 1 was taught, retaught, documented, and communicated to family.
  • Select the least intensive support likely to work; name owner and review date.
5.3

Implement Tier 2 CICO & DBRC

  • Family partnership meeting required before support begins.
  • Define 1–3 positively stated goals; assign a mentor.
  • Review weekly data — continue, revise, fade, exit, or intensify.
5.4–5.6

Tier 3, Behavior Contracts & FBA/BIP

  • Select the least intensive effective intensive tool.
  • Write expectations in observable, positive language; name adult supports.
  • FBA/BIP only after Tier 1/2 fidelity — never to delay safety support.
5.7

Attendance Support Pathway

  • Review absence data daily; ask willful vs. barrier-based.
  • Route transportation, housing, medical, or safety barriers to the correct support.
  • Create Attendance Success Agreements when a formal plan is needed.
5.8–5.9

Wellness, Safety & DCS Reporting

  • Keep the student supervised and calm; never leave alone if self-harm is suspected.
  • Notify counselor and administrator immediately.
  • Follow required screening, safety plan, or reporting process; document within required timeline.
5.10

Title IX / Protected-Status Concerns

  • Receive the report without promising an outcome; keep students safe and separate.
  • Notify the designated coordinator immediately; avoid informal investigation.
  • Follow required notice, investigation, outcome, and recordkeeping.
5.11–5.12

McKinney-Vento, Foster Care & Aspire

  • Enroll or maintain access without unnecessary delay.
  • Notify the designated liaison within the required timeframe.
  • Coordinate community-partner referrals with family consent.
5.13–5.14

OSS, Due Process, Expulsion & Missing/Withdrawn Students

  • Check disability, IEP, 504 status before formal action proceeds.
  • Provide required notice and due-process steps; plan re-entry before return.
  • Missing student: in-building sweep, then parent + law enforcement per procedure within the required window.
Part 6

Govern, Coach & Improve

Culture systems fail when adults improvise independently. Governance means a predictable rhythm for noticing patterns, making decisions, assigning owners, coaching adult practice, protecting records, and improving implementation over time.

Operating standardA meeting that does not produce an owner, action, due date, and review date is a discussion, not an operating decision.
6.1

Daily Culture Operating Rhythm

  • Morning: attendance, prior-day incidents, safety/wellness concerns, proactive check-ins.
  • Midday: DBRC completion, restorative patterns, urgent needs.
  • Before dismissal: close urgent documentation, assign next-day follow-up.
6.2

Weekly Culture/MTSS Meeting

  • Open with the clearest data truth of the week; name patterns before solutions.
  • Decide continue, revise, intensify, fade, or close for every reviewed student.
  • Assign one owner, due date, and review date per action.
6.3–6.4

Culture Deep-Dive & SRR/R3 Coaching

  • Select one focus area monthly: routine, space, grade band, or adult practice.
  • Observe the adult move, not only student behavior.
  • Name one high-leverage action step; follow up on a set date.
6.5–6.6

Data Review & Documentation Quality

  • Review LiveSchool, GiveThx, restorative, and attendance data by setting, adult, and student group.
  • Identify adult practice changes before adding student consequences.
  • Audit documentation weekly for observable, bias-free language.
6.7–6.8

Privacy & Annual System Maintenance

  • Share only what the receiving adult needs to know.
  • Store sensitive records in restricted, approved locations.
  • Refresh roles, platforms, and training before school starts each year.
