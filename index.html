Portrait of a Graduate / Competencies▾

Five competencies define the knowledge, skills, and dispositions every student develops. Each is paired with what mastery looks like in practice.

Academic Ownership

Students take responsibility for their learning by setting goals, monitoring progress, and advocating for support. Mastery looks like: tracking their own data, identifying where they are stuck, asking for help before a problem grows, and articulating learning goals unprompted.

Collaboration

Students work effectively with others by contributing strengths, listening actively, and supporting shared goals. Mastery looks like: sharing work fairly, staying engaged when others lead, giving honest and kind feedback, and holding up their end of group commitments.

Communication

Students express ideas clearly across formats and audiences, and listen to understand. Mastery looks like: adjusting tone and format for the audience, listening without interrupting, and explaining thinking in writing, conversation, and presentation.

Honor

Students act with integrity, respect themselves and others, and take responsibility for their impact. Mastery looks like: telling the truth even when it is hard, treating peers and adults with consistent respect, and owning mistakes without deflecting.

Resilience

Students persist through challenges, learn from mistakes, and adapt their approach. Mastery looks like: trying a new strategy when the first fails, recovering from a low grade without shutting down, and reflecting on what to do differently.

Alignment to Instructional Systems
  • Competencies are embedded in daily instruction — not a separate behavior system. Teachers reference competency language during lessons, transitions, and feedback.
  • Student-led conferences are the primary formal structure for competency demonstration, each semester.
  • Competency progress is tracked alongside academic data through observation, artifacts, and conferencing data logged in Matchbook’s progress monitoring system.
  • Lesson design connects competency expectations to content standards explicitly — students know which competency they are practicing and why.
  • Competency language lives in the student handbook, family-facing materials, and classroom norms for consistency across settings.

Professional Practice & Staff Development

How we support staff development and maintain consistent professional expectations across the school.

Our Commitment to Staff Development▾

At Matchbook Learning, we believe that excellent teaching is developed through intentional practice, meaningful feedback, and continuous reflection. Professional growth is an expectation for every educator, regardless of years of experience or current level of performance.

Our approach to staff development is built on coaching, collaboration, and shared accountability. We are committed to creating a culture where feedback is welcomed, growth is celebrated, and every staff member has the support needed to continuously improve their practice in service of student success.

We also believe that teaching should be deeply meaningful and joyful. While the work is demanding, we strive to create a school where educators feel supported, appreciated, and inspired by the difference they make each day. We celebrate progress, recognize excellence, and believe that investing in our people is one of the most important ways we invest in our students.

Coaching▾

Culture of Coaching

Instructional coaching is a core component of professional growth at Matchbook Learning and an expectation for all teachers. Coaching is universal — it is not optional, nor is it reserved for struggling teachers. Every educator participates in coaching because strong instruction is developed through consistent feedback, reflection, and implementation over time.

At Matchbook, coaching is rooted in partnership before oversight. Like the relationship between a resident and an attending physician, coaches meet teachers where they are, recognize strengths, and provide the precise guidance needed to accelerate growth. The purpose of coaching is developmental rather than punitive. Coaches are not cataloging mistakes — they are identifying the highest-leverage instructional moves that will have the greatest impact on student learning.

Strong teaching is not achieved in isolation. It is cultivated through ongoing collaboration, meaningful feedback, and a shared commitment to continuous improvement. Our goal is not simply to improve instructional practice, but to build confident educators who find purpose, fulfillment, and joy in helping students grow.

Coaching Cycles

Every teacher participates in ongoing coaching cycles that may include:

  • Goal-setting conversations
  • Classroom observations
  • Feedback meetings
  • One clear, actionable next step
  • Follow-up observations and support

Coaching may occur in a variety of ways depending on teacher needs and instructional goals, including:

Teachers can expect feedback that is:

  • Timely
  • Specific
  • Actionable

  • Focused on one high-leverage instructional action step

Every coaching conversation includes:

Grow

Whenever possible, coaches utilize common language from Teach Like a Champion (TLaC), Get Better Faster (GBF) and RELAY Graduate School of Education to ensure consistency across coaching experiences.

Professional Growth Expectations▾

Professional growth requires active participation from every staff member. Coaching is most effective when teachers actively engage in the process and view feedback as an opportunity for continuous improvement.

Teachers are expected to:

  • Participate fully in coaching cycles.
  • Implement agreed-upon action steps.
  • Reflect on instructional practice.
  • Arrive prepared for coaching conversations.
  • Seek support when needed.
  • Collaborate professionally with coaches and colleagues.
  • Maintain a growth mindset and a willingness to continuously refine instructional practice.

We believe professional growth is strengthened when we celebrate successes, encourage one another, and contribute to a positive school culture where both adults and students enjoy coming to school each day.

Evaluation System▾

At Matchbook Learning, coaching and evaluation serve complementary but distinct purposes. Coaching is developmental and ongoing, while evaluations provide a formal measure of instructional effectiveness and professional responsibilities.

Teachers are evaluated using the Matchbook Learning Teacher Evaluation Rubric, which is aligned to our instructional framework and reflects the practices that lead to strong student outcomes. Evaluations may include evidence gathered through:

  • Formal observations
  • Informal walkthroughs
  • Coaching cycles
  • Professional responsibilities
  • Student learning evidence
  • Progress toward professional goals

Instructional coaching is focused on supporting growth, reflection, and the ongoing development of strong instructional practice. Coaching is grounded in partnership and is designed to help teachers refine their craft over time in a supportive space. Instructional coaches are not evaluators; coaching conversations are separate from the formal evaluation process. Coaches may, however, use the evaluation tool as a shared reference point to align feedback, highlight strengths, and identify next steps connected to schoolwide expectations.

At the beginning of each school year, teachers should become familiar with the evaluation rubric and performance expectations. This shared understanding helps ensure clarity around what strong instruction looks like and supports more focused, consistent coaching throughout the year.

Academics & Instruction

How we plan, teach, assess, and respond to ensure strong student learning and growth.

Instructional Philosophy▾

Great instruction is planned, joyful, and relentlessly focused on student outcomes. At Matchbook Learning, excellent teaching is prepared in advance, informed by data, and executed with precision and warmth. We start with the end in mind: we begin by unpacking the unit assessment to name the knowledge, skills, and reasoning students must demonstrate. This is not teaching to the test — it is planning toward a clear target so we can map the most direct route to get every student there. And when the data tells us students are not arriving, we adjust. Instruction that does not respond to evidence is not instruction; it is repetition.

Curriculum fidelity is the foundation of that route. Our curricula are research-based and vertically aligned, ensuring every student receives coherent, rigorous instruction regardless of classroom. Matchbook teachers follow the curriculum with integrity, freeing themselves from designing lessons from scratch to do the deeper work instead: internalizing the content, anticipating misconceptions, and responding to what data tells them in real time. Consistency across classrooms is equity — it guarantees that every student builds on the same evidence-based foundation.

Planning is where instruction begins — not a separate task that precedes it, but the first act of teaching itself. Strong outcomes are not a product of good intentions — they are a product of tight plans. The real work is internalization: knowing your lesson so thoroughly that you can hold the content, the students, and the room all at once. Tight, well-scripted planning builds the muscle memory that allows a teacher to eventually hold the structure in their head rather than on the page. As instruction strengthens and planning becomes habitual, the rigidity of planning can loosen — because the foundation has already been built.

Pacing Guide Expectations

See the Pacing Guide 2026–27. To understand the pacing guide’s design, start with the assessments.

  1. Internal (curriculum) and external (state, benchmark) assessment dates are set on the pacing guide and are rigid.
  2. ELA takes Bi-Weekly Assessments (BWAs / progress check-ups) on a bi-weekly basis to alternate with Math Topic assessments, preventing multiple assessments in one week.
  3. Checkpoints can be retaken after the initial assessments. DOE sets the window for Checkpoints 1, 2, and 3; Matchbook may set dates for subsequent retests based on when standards are taught. A Checkpoint 1 retake is given after all assessed standards have been taught.
  4. Flexible days are built in to absorb unforeseen interruptions (weather, testing pulls) and maintain pacing. Unused Flex Days can be used for reteaching or to get ahead. Flex Days are not free/reward days — lesson plans are still required.
  5. Standards and IDOE Item Specifications are identified for each unit — your North Star when planning and internalizing.
  6. Deviations from the Pacing Guide require approval from the Director of Academics.
Lesson Planning Expectations▾

Submission

Plans are due in GROW every Wednesday by 5:00 PM. Plans should not be submitted during school breaks — submit before the break begins.

Why It Matters

Planning in advance lets SpEd teachers preview content and prepare accommodations, MLL specialists anticipate language demands, and coaches offer meaningful feedback before instruction — not after. Submit by Wednesday and every student gets your best lesson.

Format Options (before gradual release)

The plan you submit is the one in your hands during instruction.

Option A — Digital Template

Paste TE content into the Matchbook template. Submit the digital file to GROW, then print for class. Do not modify the template without first consulting your coach. Examples: 8th Math, 6th Reading, 2nd Math, 2nd Reading. Template.

Option B — Annotated TE

Hand-annotate your physical TE. Scan and submit the PDF to GROW by Wednesday. Annotations must address all required elements. Examples: 8th Math, 6th Reading, 2nd Math, 2nd Reading.

Small Group Planning Template

One document for the whole week; format is flexible. Submit separately in GROW or in the same document as core plans. Must include: the teacher-led activity, the independent activity, and who is in each group.

!

At Matchbook, we encourage teachers to use AI tools to work more efficiently — anything that returns time to teachers is a win. But there is a slippery slope between using AI to work smarter and using AI to check a box. A lesson plan is not a deliverable for leadership; it is a tool for the teacher. If it is generated, submitted, and never internalized, it is not a lesson plan — it’s paperwork with better formatting. Your lesson is expected to be in your hands during instruction, and what is on the page is expected to live in the room. If it becomes clear a teacher has not internalized their AI-generated plans, they will complete lesson internalizations by hand until that foundation is built.

!

A PowerPoint slide deck is not a lesson plan. It is a supplementary material you may prepare to support lesson execution, but it is not required.

Scanning From Your Phone

CamScanner, Apple Notes (camera → Scan Documents), or Adobe Scan all work well. Combine all pages into one PDF before uploading.

If You Can’t Meet the Deadline

Reach out to your Instructional Coach and Director of Academics before 5:00 PM on Wednesday. Contacting someone after the deadline is not advance notice. Proactive communication is the Matchbook standard.

Data & Assessment

Data and assessment serve as the foundation for instructional decision-making. We use multiple sources of data to understand student progress, identify gaps, and guide intervention and enrichment in real time — formative, interim, and summative measures consistently analyzed at the classroom, grade, and school level to keep instruction responsive and targeted.

Grades & Grading Policy

Grades represent an ongoing and accurate reflection of a student’s current level of mastery of grade-level standards. They are not a completion record, but a measure of academic understanding demonstrated through consistent performance over time. Grades must be updated frequently, with missing-assignment reports shared with families weekly to ensure transparency and partnership.

State Assessments▾

Required or state-adopted measures used to evaluate student mastery of Indiana Academic Standards and English language proficiency.

Acadience (K–2 Literacy)

Foundational reading assessment measuring phonemic awareness, decoding, fluency, and comprehension. Used for screening, intervention placement, and ongoing progress monitoring.

Forefront (K–2 Math)

Standards-aligned system measuring mastery of priority standards throughout the year, providing timely data between major benchmark windows.

IREAD (Grades 2–3)

Adaptive literacy assessment focused on phonics, vocabulary, fluency, and comprehension; supports early identification and targeted intervention.

WIDA ACCESS (K–8 English Learners)

English language proficiency assessment measuring listening, speaking, reading, and writing; results guide language supports and scaffolding.

I AM (Grades 3–8 Special Education)

Indiana’s alternate assessment aligned to alternate achievement standards for students with significant cognitive disabilities.

ILEARN (Grades 3–8)

Indiana’s statewide summative assessment in ELA, Math, and Science (where applicable) — the primary accountability measure for grade-level mastery.

ILEARN Checkpoints & Retakes (Grades 3–8)

Interim assessments that measure progress toward mastery and provide actionable data to guide instruction. Retakes follow intervention and reteaching, supporting a growth-based model rather than a single-point-in-time measure.

Additional Assessments (Normed & Internal)▾

These provide comparative growth data, cognitive insights, and ongoing progress monitoring.

NWEA MAP Growth (K–8)

Nationally normed, computer-adaptive assessment measuring growth in reading and math. K–2: administered BOY, MOY, EOY. 3–8: BOY baselines and growth toward end-of-year targets.

CogAT (typically Grades 2 & 5)

Cognitive abilities assessment measuring verbal, quantitative, and nonverbal reasoning; supports identification of advanced learners and placement.

ClearSight Progress Monitoring (K–8 as applicable)

Diagnostic and progress-monitoring tool to track skill development in real time and support instructional adjustments between benchmarks.

Section 04

Schoolwide Culture & Behavior Systems

How we build a safe, supportive, predictable environment through prevention, recognition, and response.

Overview: Prevention · Recognition · Response▾

School culture is built intentionally through proactive systems, positive recognition, and consistent responses to behavior. Our goal is a safe, supportive, and predictable environment where every student can thrive academically, socially, and emotionally. Our approach is organized into three interconnected areas:

  • Prevention — teaching and reinforcing expectations before problems occur.
  • Recognition — celebrating and acknowledging positive behavior.
  • Response — providing appropriate support and intervention when students need additional assistance.

Together, these systems ensure students know what is expected, feel valued for meeting expectations, and receive the support necessary for success.

Culture Philosophy▾

Safe, Respectful, Responsible

The foundation of school culture is the expectation that all students and staff operate within a safe, respectful, and responsible environment at all times. These expectations are universal across all settings; explicitly taught, modeled, and reinforced; applied consistently by all staff; and embedded into daily routines and interactions.

Adult Responsibility for Culture

Creating and maintaining a positive culture is the responsibility of every staff member. Staff explicitly teach expectations, model appropriate behavior, reinforce positive choices, and respond consistently to misbehavior.

Student Accountability

Students are expected to meet behavior expectations in every environment. Accountability is predictable and fair, grounded in schoolwide expectations, and reinforced daily through consistent adult practices.

Code of Conduct

Matchbook Learning operates in alignment with the Indianapolis Public Schools (IPS) Code of Conduct, which outlines student rights and responsibilities, behaviors subject to disciplinary action, and fair, consistent procedures. Reference: IPS Code of Conduct.

Consistent Schoolwide Language

All staff use shared language when reinforcing expectations, correcting behavior, and recognizing success, so students experience the same expectations regardless of setting or staff member.

Prevention Systems▾

Prevention systems establish clear expectations and routines before behavioral concerns arise.

Schoolwide Expectations & Behavior Matrix

Behavior matrices define what it means to be Safe, Respectful, and Responsible in every setting (classrooms, hallways, cafeteria, playground, restrooms). Expectations are explicitly taught, modeled, practiced, and revisited throughout the year. Reference: Schoolwide Behavior Matrix.

Hall Pass Procedures

Hall pass systems provide accountability and monitor movement during instructional time — requesting a pass, traveling appropriately, and returning promptly. Reference: Hall Pass PDF.

Dress Code & Items of Non-Compliance

Clear expectations regarding dress and personal items help maintain focused, distraction-free learning. Items of non-compliance include cell phones and personal electronics, earbuds and smartwatches, toys, trading cards, food/beverages outside designated times, blankets/pillows/purses/non-clear backpacks, and clothing that violates dress code. Students should be provided opportunities to correct concerns whenever possible, with support from the Family Engagement Coordinator, access to replacement clothing, and administrative support when necessary.

Morning Meeting

A daily Tier 1 culture-building routine that reinforces schoolwide expectations, builds relationships, strengthens community, promotes SEL, establishes readiness for learning, celebrates successes, and addresses classroom culture proactively. Considered a universal support for all students.

Regulation Tools (Calm Down Kits)

Tier 1 preventative supports that help students regulate emotions, reset and re-engage, and return successfully to instruction. They are not consequences or removals from instruction, are intended for brief use, and support self-regulation.

Classroom Systems & Routines

Teachers establish procedures that maximize instructional time and promote independence: entry/dismissal, transitions, voice levels, attention signals, independent work, partner/group discussions, materials management, and technology procedures.

Celebration & Recognition Systems▾

Positive behavior is intentionally acknowledged and reinforced. Recognition should be immediate, specific, and connected to expectations.

Individual Recognition

  • LiveSchool — points recognize behaviors aligned to Safe, Respectful, Responsible expectations and Cardinal Character traits. Points are never deducted and may be redeemed for approved rewards throughout the year.
  • Cardinal Character Awards — celebrate the values of CARDINALS: Character, Achievement, Resilience, Determination, Integrity, Nurturing, Acceptance, Leadership, Service. Monthly recognitions highlight one trait at a time, culminating in All-In Cardinal honors.
  • Honor Roll — recognizes consistent academic excellence based on quarterly report card grades.
  • Perfect Attendance — celebrates students with no absences during the quarter.

Collective Recognition

  • Brag Boards — classes earn recognition for excellent hallway transitions, strong compliance, and consistent Safe/Respectful/Responsible behavior, working toward a class-selected reward.
  • Attendance Celebrations — homerooms earn letters toward the word ATTENDANCE for perfect attendance.
  • LiveSchool House Points — students belong to vertical houses (one homeroom section from every grade level); competitions promote collaboration, school pride, leadership, cross-grade relationships, and belonging.

504 Plans▾

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 prohibits discrimination on the basis of disability and ensures eligible students have equal access to educational programs and activities. A Section 504 Plan provides accommodations and supports that allow a qualified student to access and participate in the general education environment.

Referral Process

Referrals must be based on objective data. Sources may include MTSS academic intervention data, behavioral intervention data and progress monitoring, DBRC data, universal screening results, attendance patterns, health information, parent concerns, and staff observations of documented educational impact. Teachers do not independently recommend or determine that a student should receive a Section 504 Plan — they provide data and observations to the appropriate school team.

Initial Team Review

The MTSS Team and Culture Team review all available academic, behavioral, attendance, and health data. The team first verifies that appropriate Tier I supports were implemented, Tier II interventions were implemented and monitored when appropriate, and data indicate barriers that may be related to a disability rather than a lack of instruction.

Medical Conditions

Students with documented medical or mental-health conditions may be considered when the condition substantially limits one or more major life activities. A medical diagnosis alone does not automatically qualify a student; decisions are based on multiple data sources and educational impact.

Evaluation & Eligibility

If warranted, parent/guardian consent is obtained when required, and relevant records, intervention data, and health information are reviewed. A student may be found eligible when the team concludes there is a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits a major life activity and requires accommodations. Decisions are made by the designated Section 504 team — not solely on teacher recommendation or a medical diagnosis.

Plan Development, Review & Documentation

An eligible student’s plan identifies documented needs, specific accommodations and supports, staff responsible for implementation, health/safety considerations, and a monitoring plan. Plans are reviewed at least annually or when needs change. All referrals, evaluations, decisions, communications, notes, plans, and reviews are maintained per school policy and federal/state confidentiality requirements.

Counselors, Wellness, Threat Assessments & Safety Plans▾

Grade-Band Counselor Responsibilities

Distinct expectations exist for K–4 and 5–8 counselors, with shared responsibilities across both bands (caseload management, crisis response, Aspire collaboration, documentation).

Aspire Indiana

Primary external mental-health partner. Referrals follow a defined process; Aspire meetings occur every other Wednesday, 2:30–3:30, between counselors and the Aspire team.

Threat Assessments

Conducted when statements, actions, behaviors, or concerns indicate a potential risk of harm to self or others — to assess risk, promote safety, determine intervention needs, and develop support plans. The Threat Assessment Team may include School Counselors, Dean, Assistant Principal, Tier III Administrator, and additional staff as needed. Documentation: Threat Assessment Forms, Threat Assessment Tracker, Safety Plans, and Parent Communication Documentation.

Safety Plans

Developed when students demonstrate significant behavioral, threat-assessment, emotional-regulation, mental-health, or safety concerns. Plans identify triggers, warning signs, prevention strategies, support staff, and parent-communication expectations. The Safety Plan Tracker is updated whenever a plan is created or revised.

!

Suicide Ideation Procedures. Any statement involving self-harm, suicide, or harm to others must be treated seriously and immediately. School counselors are responsible for conducting suicide risk assessments, contacting parents immediately, developing safety plans when appropriate, connecting families to resources, and documenting interventions. The school’s Suicide Ideation Protocol must be followed for all incidents.

Section 06

Special Education

How we identify and serve students with disabilities in accordance with IDEA and Indiana Article 7.

Referral & Evaluation Process▾

Matchbook Learning is committed to identifying and serving students with disabilities in accordance with the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) and Indiana Article 7.

Child Find & Referral

  1. Students experiencing academic, behavioral, communication, developmental, or functional difficulties are first supported through the school’s MTSS.
  2. General education interventions, progress-monitoring data, and parent input are documented prior to referral when appropriate. A referral is not delayed when a disability is suspected.
  3. Referrals for an initial evaluation may be made by parents/guardians, school personnel, or outside agencies/service providers.
  4. All referrals are submitted to the Special Education Coordinator for review.
  5. The referral team reviews existing data and determines whether additional information is necessary to suspect a disability and proceed with an educational evaluation.

Evaluation Timelines

  • Within ten (10) instructional days of receiving a referral, the school provides parents written notice regarding the proposed evaluation.
  • Once written parental consent is obtained, the initial evaluation is completed within fifty (50) instructional days per Indiana Article 7.
  • Evaluation reports are completed and reviewed by the multidisciplinary team prior to the eligibility conference.
Speech/OT vs. Full Educational Evaluation▾

Speech/Language or Occupational Therapy Evaluation

May be considered when concerns are isolated to communication, fine motor, sensory, or functional performance — e.g., articulation or language concerns; stuttering or voice concerns; fine-motor deficits impacting performance; sensory or self-regulation concerns affecting participation. The Coordinator, related-service provider, and evaluation team determine whether a related-service evaluation alone is appropriate or whether a comprehensive evaluation is warranted.

Full Educational Evaluation

Conducted when there is suspicion of a disability that may require specially designed instruction. May include academic achievement testing, cognitive assessment, social/emotional and behavioral assessment, adaptive behavior measures, classroom observations, parent and teacher input, and speech/language and OT assessments when appropriate. Eligibility determinations are made by the Case Conference Committee (CCC) using all available data.

IEP Development & Case Conference Expectations▾

The Case Conference Committee (CCC) develops, reviews, and revises each student’s Individualized Education Program (IEP).

Required Participants

Parent/guardian; general education teacher; special education teacher/Teacher of Record (TOR); school administrator or designee; individuals able to interpret evaluation results when appropriate; and the student when transition services are discussed.

IEP Development Expectations — Teachers of Record shall

  1. Prepare draft IEP documents prior to meetings.
  2. Collect and review current progress-monitoring data, classroom performance, assessment results, and teacher input.
  3. Ensure PLAAFPs (Present Levels) are current and data-based.
  4. Develop measurable annual goals aligned to identified needs.
  5. Clearly document accommodations, modifications, services, and supports.
  6. Maintain compliance with all state and federal timelines.

Case Conference Expectations

  • Annual case conferences occur at least once every twelve (12) months.
  • Reevaluations occur at least every three (3) years unless waived by the CCC.
  • Move-in case conferences are convened within ten (10) instructional days of enrollment when students transfer with an existing IEP.
  • Meeting notices are provided per procedural safeguards; staff arrive prepared with current data and actively participate in collaborative problem-solving.
Inclusion Model Expectations▾

Matchbook Learning uses an inclusive service-delivery model whenever appropriate so students with disabilities access grade-level curriculum alongside nondisabled peers.

General Education Teachers shall

  • Assume shared ownership for all students, including those receiving special education services;
  • Implement IEP accommodations and modifications with fidelity;
  • Collaborate regularly with special education staff;
  • Provide access to grade-level instruction and classroom activities;
  • Differentiate using Universal Design for Learning (UDL) principles;
  • Participate in IEP meetings and progress-monitoring activities.

Special Education Staff shall

  • Collaborate with classroom teachers during planning and instruction;
  • Provide specially designed instruction as outlined in the IEP;
  • Support accommodations and instructional differentiation;
  • Monitor student progress and adjust interventions;
  • Promote student independence and self-advocacy.

Removal from the general education setting occurs only when the nature or severity of the disability prevents satisfactory progress even with supplementary aids and services.

Resource Room & DREAM Program▾

Resource Room Services

Specially designed instruction in small-group settings for students requiring intensive academic support beyond the general education classroom — explicit instruction in reading, writing, mathematics, and executive functioning; remediation of skill deficits; pre-teaching or re-teaching grade-level concepts; and progress monitoring aligned to IEP goals. Service minutes are provided per each student’s IEP.

DREAM Program

Serves students with significant cognitive, adaptive, communication, and/or functional needs requiring a highly specialized environment — functional academics, communication instruction, SEL, daily-living skills, behavioral supports, community-based instruction when appropriate, and individualized transition planning. Students continue to access inclusive opportunities with nondisabled peers to the maximum extent appropriate.

Accommodations & Progress Monitoring▾

Accommodations Implementation with Fidelity

  1. Teachers review accommodation plans at the beginning of each year and whenever revisions occur.
  2. Accommodations are implemented consistently across all instructional settings, assessments, extracurriculars, and school-sponsored events.
  3. Staff document concerns and communicate with the Teacher of Record immediately if accommodations appear ineffective.
  4. Accommodations may not be added, removed, or altered without CCC approval.
  5. The Coordinator conducts periodic compliance reviews.
!

Failure to implement accommodations as written may constitute a denial of a Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE).

Progress Monitoring Tied to IEP Goals

  • Teachers of Record collect data on every IEP goal per the IEP schedule.
  • Data are objective, measurable, and aligned to baseline and annual goals.
  • Evidence may include curriculum-based measures, work samples, assessment data, behavioral data, observation records, frequency counts, and service logs.
  • Progress reports are provided to parents at least as frequently as report cards for nondisabled students.
  • The Coordinator reviews documentation quarterly. When insufficient progress is shown, the CCC may reconvene to revise the IEP.
Functional Behavioral Assessment (FBA) & BIP▾

An FBA is a systematic process used to identify the function or purpose of challenging behaviors. It may be considered when a student’s behavior significantly interferes with learning, a pattern of repeated disciplinary incidents exists, behavioral interventions have been unsuccessful, or the CCC determines additional supports are necessary.

The FBA process includes review of existing data; parent and staff interviews; direct observations; collection and analysis of behavior data; and identification of antecedents, behaviors, consequences, and behavioral function. Results are used to develop or revise a Behavior Intervention Plan (BIP), which includes preventative strategies, clearly defined replacement behaviors, reinforcement systems, staff responsibilities, and data-collection procedures.

Transition Planning▾

Elementary to Middle School

The Special Education Team reviews needs, accommodations, and supports; conducts transition meetings between sending and receiving staff; shares current IEPs, behavior plans, and intervention data; provides student orientation to new settings; and communicates transition information with families.

Middle School to High School

Beginning no later than age fourteen (14), transition services are addressed annually within the IEP per Indiana Article 7. Planning includes student strengths, preferences, and interests; postsecondary education and career goals; employment goals; independent-living goals when appropriate; courses of study aligned to postsecondary goals; and transition services and activities needed to achieve them. Planning involves students, families, school staff, and outside agencies when appropriate.
